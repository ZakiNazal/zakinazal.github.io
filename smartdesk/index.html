How a fault report
becomes a deadline

A helpdesk ticket usually arrives as free text and a shrug. The person reporting knows what broke; they don't know how urgent it is next to everything else in the queue, or who can fix it. SmartDesk's job is to answer those two questions before a human reads the ticket at all.

1 · Deflect before you route

The cheapest ticket is the one that never gets filed. Before anything is queued, the description and category are tokenised and matched against the knowledge base: one point per keyword hit, a point and a half if the category matches outright, then normalised by the article's own keyword count so a long article can't win on volume alone.

If nothing clears a confidence of 0.18, it doesn't guess. It says so, and routes the ticket with everything the user already typed. A wrong guided fix costs more than no guided fix — it wastes the user's time and still ends in a ticket.

2 · Score the signals, not the sentiment

Priority starts at 20 and earns its way up. Every ticket carries an urgency the user picked and a context they picked, and those are worth different amounts — an exam outranks office work, because an exam has a hard stop.

Weights applied to a new ticket
Base 20
Urgency Blocking +35 · High +22 · Normal +8 · Low +0
Context Exam +30 · In class +24 · Lab +18 · Deadline +14 · Office +6
Blast radius +15 when the text names more than one person affected
Shared kit +8 for Wi-Fi or classroom equipment

The total is clamped to 100 and cut into four bands: 75+ critical, 55+ high, 30+ medium, otherwise low. The score is stored alongside the reason it earned — "user is fully blocked, time-critical exam context" — so a technician can see why the queue is ordered the way it is, and disagree with it.

A note on honesty: this is weighted heuristics, not a model. The code calls the module AiEngine, and the file says plainly that the functions are kept small so a real model could replace them later. Nothing here is learned from data, and the case study shouldn't imply otherwise.

3 · The score is a clock, not a label

Priority that only changes a badge colour is decoration. Here it sets a deadline the moment the ticket is created — dueAt = now + slaHours, where critical buys 4 hours, high 8, medium 24, low 72.

From then on the ticket reports its own state: breached once the deadline has passed, at risk once less than a quarter of its budget is left, otherwise fine. Because "at risk" is a proportion rather than a fixed window, a 4-hour critical starts warning after three hours and a 72-hour low doesn't panic on day one.

4 · Route by who can, then by who's free

Assignment filters to technicians whose skills list covers the category, then sorts that pool by open ticket count and takes the lightest. If nobody has the skill, the pool widens to everyone rather than leaving the ticket unassigned — a slow fix beats a silent queue.

When it needs a vendor, the ticket crosses into a second, smaller state machine — received, diagnosed, awaiting approval, in repair, completed — so external work has its own vocabulary instead of overloading the internal statuses.

5 · Notice what keeps breaking

Every ticket is grouped by category and location. Three or more in the same bucket is promoted to a recurring fault and surfaced to managers. This is the part I'd defend hardest: a projector that fails once is a ticket, and a projector that fails every week is a purchasing decision. Closing the same ticket repeatedly looks like productivity and isn't.

What I'd change

The weights are constants in a Dart file. They were tuned by argument, not evidence, and there's no way to adjust them without a rebuild — they belong in remote config, so a manager can decide an exam is worth more than I decided it was. Nothing measures whether the scoring is any good, either: the data to check it is all there in resolution times, and I never closed that loop.
