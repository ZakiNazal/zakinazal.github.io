Case study · QBooking · Flutter · Collaboration

Three portals,
one app

QBooking is a booking platform with three kinds of user — customers, shop owners, and staff — and each sees a different product. The companion app has to work out which one you are before it can show you anything, and be graceful when it turns out you thought you were someone else.

I joined this as a collaborator, working alongside another developer, so I'm scoping this to the parts I wrote: role routing, the three portal shells, session restore, and the shared widget set. The backend and the web portal are not mine.

1 · The app doesn't know who you are yet

An account resolves to one of four actor types — customer, owner, staff, or unresolved. That fourth one is the important one. The type arrives from the backend on a wire field, and "we haven't been told yet" is a real state the UI has to survive, not an error to swallow. Modelling it explicitly means the splash screen can wait honestly instead of guessing and correcting itself a second later.

2 · When you picked the wrong door

Login asks which portal you want. That answer is only a hint — the account decides what it actually opens. When the two disagree, the obvious implementations are both bad: an error implies your credentials were wrong, and silently redirecting leaves you somewhere you didn't ask to be, wondering if you're in someone else's account.

So the mismatch gets its own screen, and it names both halves of the discrepancy: "You selected Staff. This account opens Owner." One button continues to the portal that actually exists, another signs out. Nothing is hidden and nothing happens without you pressing something.

This is the piece of the app I'd defend hardest in review. It's a screen most users will never see, for a state that only occurs when someone is confused — which is exactly when an app is least allowed to be confusing back.

3 · Restoring a session, but not forever

Staying signed in means the app resumes your session on launch. That call goes over the network, and a network call with no ceiling is a splash screen that can hang indefinitely on bad hotel Wi-Fi.

Session restore is bounded at twelve seconds. Past that it stops waiting and says so — "Could not restore your session. Please sign in again." Signing in again is a mild annoyance; a spinner with no end is the app being broken. Twelve seconds is long enough that a slow-but-working connection still gets through.

4 · Three shells from one set of parts

Customer, owner and staff each get their own shell, but they're assembled from one shared widget library rather than three parallel implementations — branding, buttons, glass cards, text fields, a segment control, section headers, a responsive scaffold, the navigation bar, and a set of state cards for empty, loading and error.

Consolidating that was most of my second week on the project, and it was worth it for a specific reason: with three portals, any component built inline is a component that will be rebuilt slightly differently twice more. The shared set is what stops the three products drifting into looking like three different apps.

What I took from it

This was the first time I'd written Flutter to someone else's brief, in someone else's repository, next to another developer — and the difference from my own projects wasn't technical. It was that I couldn't hold the whole thing in my head, so decisions had to be legible to somebody who wasn't me. Naming an unresolved state, giving a mismatch its own screen, and putting a number on how long a wait is allowed to last are all the same instinct: write down the thing you'd otherwise have to remember.
