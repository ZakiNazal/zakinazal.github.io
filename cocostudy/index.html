what you forget

Turning a lecture into flashcards is the easy half. The hard half is deciding when to show each card again — and then not lying to the person about how well they're doing.

1 · The scheduler

Every card carries an ease factor, an interval, and a state. Grading a card moves all three: ease drifts down 0.2 when you fail and up 0.15 when it was easy, clamped between 1.3 and 2.8 so one bad morning can't poison a card forever and one lucky run can't push it out of sight.

What a grade does to the interval
Again Interval to zero, back in 10 minutes, card marked lapsed
Hard × 1.2, and a new or lapsed card enters learning
Good × ease, or 1 day if it's the card's first pass
Easy × ease × 1.3, or 3 days from new

Intervals cap at a year. The multiplier uses the ease after the grade is applied, not before — otherwise Easy doesn't compound the way the rest of the maths assumes, and the card you know best is the one that comes back soonest.

2 · The progress bar was lying

Mastery is shown as highlighter ink: nothing, pink while learning, yellow in review, green once a card's interval reaches 21 days. Set-level progress originally averaged that coverage across the deck — a learning card counted as a third mastered, a reviewing one as two thirds.

A deck of ten with three genuinely mature cards reported 56% under a label reading "Mastered". It felt encouraging and it was wrong, which is the worst combination in a tool someone is trusting to tell them whether they're ready for an exam. It now counts only cards that are actually mature.

I've kept this one in the write-up because the bug was in the arithmetic being flattering, not in the arithmetic being broken. Nothing would have crashed. No test I'd have thought to write beforehand would have caught it.

3 · Your own backup is untrusted input

Everything lives in IndexedDB on your machine, so export and import are the only way work moves between devices — which makes the restore path the most dangerous code in the project. A person picks a file off disk. It might be from an older version, a newer one, or something that isn't a CocoStudy export at all.

So the importer validates field by field rather than trusting the shape: every string, date and scheduling record is checked and given a fallback, and a card whose scheduling state doesn't survive inspection is reset to new rather than dropped or allowed through malformed. A restore merges into what's already there instead of replacing it, because the failure mode of "your file was slightly wrong" should never be "your existing decks are gone".

4 · Getting the material in

Input is whatever a student actually has: PDFs, Word documents, PowerPoint, or audio. Word files go through a converter to HTML and back down to Markdown; slide decks are unzipped and read; audio is base64-encoded and passed through as-is. Anything unrecognised raises a typed error that names the file and lists what would work, rather than failing silently.

Notes render as Markdown with GitHub tables, LaTeX maths, and generated heading anchors, so a chemistry deck and a linear algebra deck both survive the round trip.

5 · Where the tests are

215 tests, concentrated almost entirely in src/lib — the scheduler, the mastery maths, the backup round trip, the schema migrations, the extractors. None of them test whether a button is blue. They test the code that decides what you see next and the code that owns your data, because those are the two places where being wrong is expensive and invisible.

What I'd change

The scheduler is SM-2 in spirit and its constants are mine by assertion — 1.2 for Hard, 1.3 for Easy, 21 days for mature. They're reasonable and they are not measured. Every review is already recorded, so the honest next step is to check whether the intervals this produces actually match when people forget, rather than trusting numbers I picked because a paper from 1987 used them.
